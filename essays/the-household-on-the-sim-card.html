The Household on the SIM Card

Indonesia told platforms to get a parent's consent and let them decide what a parent looks like, and they have settled for any adult. The state gave its own answer at the phone counter, where a child's new SIM needs the face of the head of household, and the child's registration is reported to the ministry every quarter.

Since 1 July, a child in Indonesia who wants a new SIM card has needed an adult's face to get one. Under the subscriber-registration rule of the Ministry of Communication and Digital Affairs, Permen Komdigi 7/2026, in full effect from that date, adults register with their national identity number, the NIK, and a live face matched against Indonesia's civil registry (Dukcapil). An unmarried applicant under 17 who has no electronic identity card and no recorded biometrics registers with their own NIK plus the NIK and face of the “kepala keluarga sesuai dengan data yang tercantum dalam Kartu Keluarga”, the head of household as recorded on the family card (Pasal 3(2)). Dukcapil records biometrics from 16 and issues the card at 17 (CNA Indonesia), so in practice this is the route for children under 16. The government presents the rule as child protection. “Melindungi identitas anak di ruang digital kini dimulai sejak proses registrasi kartu SIM,” says its information portal, protecting a child's identity online now begins at SIM registration, and the rule is meant to ensure “pendampingan orang tua”, a parent's guidance (Indonesia Baik). The regulation itself never uses the word for parent. On 7 July a ministry director, Dany Suwardany, slid from parent to household head within one sentence, saying children register “menggunakan NIK anak, kemudian NIK orangtuanya, dan foto wajah dari kepala keluarganya”, with the child's NIK, then the parents' NIK, and the face of the head of the family (Kompas).

What is at stake is where that answer goes. Indonesia's child-safety regulation asks the same question, whose parent this is, as a matter of consent, and it leaves the answer with platforms and requires none of it to reach the state. The SIM rule sends the registration there. Operators must keep subscriber data while a line is active and for at least three months after it lapses, with no maximum in the rule itself. Every quarter they must report every active individual subscriber to the ministry's director general, “termasuk”, including, the unmarried under-17s registered through the head of household, and each entry “paling sedikit memuat”, must contain at least, the registrant's identity and number (Pasal 25). Their subscriber data centres must connect in real time to the ministry's registration monitoring system. Identities registered through the adult route must be handed over on request to the Attorney General or the police chief, to investigators, to the minister (for telecommunications policy), to the civil registry and to “instansi pemerintah lain”, other government agencies, as law provides (Pasal 13). Because the regulation defines a child's registration identity to include the head of household's NIK, I infer that the quarterly report carries it, though no annex specifies the report's fields. The face itself, the ministry says, goes to Dukcapil, and the operator gets back only a match or no-match (Antara). What the operator keeps, and on my reading reports, is a child's NIK paired with a named adult's. Platforms still lack a parent check, so the operators holding that record may one day sell them one.

The consent regulation is PP 17/2025, known as PP Tunas, which requires a platform to obtain a parent's or guardian's consent before a child under 17 uses any product, service or feature. What counts as verifying a parent it leaves open. “Yang bisa meregulasi ini adalah platform dengan solusi teknologi yang mereka miliki,” the deputy minister, Nezar Patria, said in July, leaving it to the platforms and the technology they own (Antara). The ministry's implementing regulation, Permen 9/2026, sets age bands for accounts held with parental consent and names no way of verifying the parent. From 16 January 2027 the data-protection law's implementing regulation, PP 33/2026, asks every controller of a child's data to verify parental consent “dengan mempertimbangkan teknologi yang tersedia”, considering available technology, which is the GDPR's formula. Platforms have until 27 March 2027 to bring their systems into line with PP Tunas (Pasal 49), age checks and parental consent included, and that date is the consent clause's real test.

None of this sits outside data protection. The data-protection law covers operators, requires that personal data be processed according to its purpose and deleted once no longer needed, and classes children's and biometric data as specific personal data. A sector rule decides what operators need, though, and this one has them keep the record while the line is active, even after the child turns 17. It also makes reporting to the ministry routine, and once a child is registered, the only words the rule spends on the registration bring it into the report. Indonesia's first checked answer to whose parent stands behind a child is therefore a household record kept, and on my reading reported, under terms written for every subscriber. Records made through the head-of-household route should carry limits of their own.

Platforms, left to answer the same question, settle for an adult, as they do everywhere. TikTok's Family Pairing has one user tap Parent and the other tap Teen (TikTok). Roblox's parent is an adult verified by ID or card (Roblox). In Indonesia it moved 23 million accounts into a children's tier automatically, most of the 28 million child accounts the minister counted at six months, and told shareholders in July it had “modified Verified Parental Consent (VPC) requirements in some countries” in Asia-Pacific, without saying what changed (Roblox). American law names the parent and gets the same result. COPPA asks for consent methods “reasonably calculated, in light of available technology, to ensure that the person providing consent is the child's parent” (16 CFR 312.5), and where the methods it lists verify anyone, they verify an adult. In 2013 the FTC refused a method built to verify “the existence of the parent-child relationship”. Texas requires app stores to verify that a parent account belongs to someone with “legal authority to make a decision on behalf of the minor” (SB 2420), and Apple's Family Sharing lets users set “any adult member in your Family Sharing group as a parent/guardian”. A platform can check that someone is an adult, and only a register of families can check that they are the parent.

The governments that check the parent do it at the phone counter. Korea has done so since June 2013. When a minor signs a mobile contract, the carrier sends the parent's and the child's names and resident registration numbers to the science ministry and the interior ministry to confirm the legal representative (SK Telecom). The science ministry said at launch that the resident register confirms the link where the parent is the registered head of household. Otherwise the parent brings documents proving the relationship, such as a family-relations certificate (KT). In 2024 Korea's communications regulator said that the same register check, used to issue i-PIN online credentials to children under 14, worked only where the legal representative was head of household, and had shut out about 80,000 applicants a year before a fix. Indonesia's rule records the head of household, who in a three-generation home can be a grandparent.

Vietnam states its design openly. Its Law on Identity 2023 has the national population database record each citizen's father, mother and legal representative, their relationship to the head of household, and their own mobile number (Art. 9). Decree 147 authenticates social-media accounts by Vietnamese phone number and has a parent register an under-16's account, and the Communist Party's Directive 57-CT/TW names the aim, to unify the identifiers of citizens, social-media users and telecom subscribers.

In Korea the parent link stayed with the carrier. Indonesia's minister, Meutya Hafid, told parliament's Commission I on 18 May that her ministry was drafting, “godok”, a re-registration of social-media users so that anyone opening an account “wajib menaruh nomor teleponnya sehingga identitasnya jelas”, must put in their phone number so their identity is clear (CNBC Indonesia). She cited disinformation, scams, online gambling and deepfakes (detik) and did not give children as a reason for the phone rule, though four days later a ministry official, Nanci Laura Sitinjak, tied the coming sign-up changes to children faking their age. The idea goes back to 2019, when the then minister, Rudiantara, argued that registered phones would expose anonymous posters (CNBC Indonesia). On 23 June the operators' association said it had seen no text (detik). On 1 September the head of the government's communication agency, Muhammad Qodari, suggested a phone number or an identity card, citing SIM registration as the model. The identity-card version would shut under-17s out of social accounts altogether, since the card is issued at 17, and so would break the chain a phone-number rule would extend. On 4 September Kompas called the plan “masih sebatas wacana”, still only an idea, and nothing has moved since. If a phone-number rule does land, a social account becomes traceable by the ministry or the police to a number and, for numbers registered since July, to a child's NIK and the head of household matched for it, which is Vietnam's chain by another route.

There is a second way the record could reach platforms, and it runs through a market. Platforms need a way to verify a parent by next March, and every new SIM registered through the household route now comes with a record, kept in operators' files, that ties it to a face-checked adult. Operators already sell checks against those files. Telkomsel's developer marketplace offers a service that lets “Government or Enterprise” learn “if NIK and MSISDN are paired according to Telkomsel Registration Process” (DigiHub). British operators launched an age-verification API in September 2025 to serve their new Online Safety Act (Liberty Global). Korea's carriers handled 2.53 billion phone-based identity checks in 2023 and, at about 40 won a check, earned an estimated 438.6 billion won from them between 2020 and mid-2024 (Sisa Journal-e). What they sell is the subscriber's own identity and age. Korea's consent rules still accept an adult (PIPA Enforcement Decree Art. 17-2), and I found no carrier product offering platforms the parent link the carriers have held since 2013. No Indonesian operator has announced an age or parent attestation either.

The costs point the same way. Each face match costs the operator Rp3,000, paid to the civil registry as non-tax state revenue under PP 10/2023. The operators' association says the real cost is about Rp200 and has asked for the fee to be cut or scrapped (detik), and the ministry puts new SIM sales at 250,000 to 300,000 a day (Biometric Update). Household-route checks carry the fee too, so operators are already paying for a record that platforms need and have not built. If they sell it on, the obvious basis under the data-protection law is explicit consent for a stated purpose, with a parent's consent where the data is a child's, and Telkomsel's endpoint already asks for a consent identifier. Because PP 33/2026 measures parental verification against available technology, a working operator product could also become the standard every platform is judged by. That would put the operators' household record underneath platform consent across the economy. Whether any of it happens is open, and the evidence so far is thin.

The strongest objection is that the reporting is ordinary compliance machinery. The ministry needs to know operators actually ran the check, and the regulation says the real-time link exists to support the accuracy of the reports. That purpose is real, and a record that the match was made would serve it, so limiting the routine report to that record would cost the audit nothing.

A second is scale. No count of under-17 registrations has been published, children use shared phones and their parents' numbers, and numbers registered before the rule stay valid. A 2022 survey by UNICEF, ECPAT and INTERPOL found that 82 percent of internet-using 12-to-17-year-olds in cities and 72 percent in rural areas had their own smartphone (Disrupting Harm), but nobody has measured whose NIK those SIMs carry. On a parent's number the child reads as an adult, which defeats an age gate, a risk two dissenting justices of Korea's Constitutional Court raised in 2015 about teenagers using phones in their parents' names (2013헌마517). The rule's terms apply to every record made through the household route, however many there turn out to be.

A third is that Indonesia has tied SIMs to the family card since 2017, when registration began requiring the family-card number. That paper link was gamed. In April 2018 Dukcapil's director general told parliament that one NIK had been misused to register 2,221,656 Indosat prepaid numbers (CNN Indonesia). The face makes the link hold, and the child-specific reporting is new.

The fourth is that Korea has run a similar design since 2013, and I found no documented leak, misuse or court case involving the parent link. That record is reassuring, and it is a record of the state checking each pair at signup and asking thereafter. Korean investigators obtain subscriber identity from carriers without a warrant, for 1,461,647 phone numbers in the second half of 2025, and since a December 2023 amendment the subscriber must be told within 30 days (Open Net). Indonesia checks at signup too, through the Dukcapil face match, then has operators report every active subscriber to the ministry each quarter, children included, and keep a live link open to it. I found no Korean equivalent of the quarterly subscriber report.

The argument can fail in four ways. The quarterly report could turn out to identify only the child as the registrant, which would narrow the stake to retention and requests under other law. Dukcapil could extend biometric enrolment below 16, so that the child's own face replaces the adult's, as enrolment at 16 already does for older teenagers. The social-account rule could be dropped, or written so that an email or a foreign number satisfies it, and seven years of talk without a text is partial evidence already. Or platforms could reach 27 March 2027 with a working parent check that runs outside the telecom record, which would make the phone counter one answer among several.

The consent clause asked the right question in public and is getting the answer consent clauses get. The state answered at the phone counter, and its answer, a child's line paired with a named adult, is kept with no maximum in the telecom rule and, on my reading, reported to the ministry each quarter. The Indonesian criticism of the SIM rule I could find, including a February Tempo newsletter, treats it as a privacy question for every subscriber and mentions the head-of-household requirement in passing. The ministry calls it child protection, and I found no critic treating it as a decision about children. The repair is small and belongs in the telecom rule itself, as a maximum retention period for records made through the head-of-household route and a purpose limit that keeps the named adult out of the routine report while leaving those records reachable where other law provides. Whatever operators might one day sell from those records would inherit the same terms. Nothing here is investment advice.
