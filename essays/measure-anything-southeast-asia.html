How to Measure Almost Anything in Southeast Asia

Douglas Hubbard wants managers to measure whatever they call intangible. Southeast Asia's digital economy shows why the harder problem is deciding which numbers to believe.

Douglas Hubbard opens How to Measure Anything with a provocation: if an uncertainty matters to a decision, it can be measured. He spends much of the book making that sentence sound less absurd. An “intangible” usually turns out to be a word nobody has defined closely enough. Ask what would look different if there were more or less of it, and you can normally find something to observe. The result will not be certainty, but it may leave you less uncertain than before.

What stayed with me was Hubbard's insistence on deciding what information is worth before collecting it. Write down the decision, the point at which the decision changes and the cost of being wrong. If every plausible answer leads to the same action, commissioning another survey or dashboard is theatre. The statistical machinery (decomposition, probability ranges, sampling and simulation) comes later. There is decent evidence that some of this can be learned. In a large geopolitical forecasting tournament, Barbara Mellers and her colleagues found that probability training improved calibration and resolution, although training was only one part of a system that also used teams and performance tracking.

The advice should travel well to Southeast Asia's digital economy. A company may be making one decision across markets with very different incomes, infrastructure and rules, using only a few years of its own history. But applying Hubbard here brings out a gap in the book. It is possible to quantify uncertainty inside a model and remain far too sure that the model belongs in the situation.

When I read a company metric, I find it useful to ask three questions. Would a different result change the decision? Is the number the outcome we care about or a stand-in for it? And what has to remain true for today's relationship to hold next year? Hubbard deals brilliantly with the first question. The other two cause most of the trouble.

Take gross merchandise value. GMV answers a narrow and legitimate question: how much commerce passed through the platform? It says much less about what the commerce was worth to the platform.

Suppose a marketplace spends one dollar on vouchers and delivery to generate a ten-dollar order. GMV has risen by ten dollars. Whether the campaign worked depends on what happens after the voucher disappears, how much revenue the platform keeps, what fulfilment costs, and how long it takes to earn back the subsidy. The same GMV can sit above a valuable cohort or a rented one. Retention, take rate, contribution and cash payback have to do the rest of the work.

Goodhart's law enters when rewards are attached to the headline. Goodhart's original point was that a statistical relationship tends to break once authorities start using it for control. Founders, managers and investors respond to incentives too. If fundraising rewards transaction volume without fully charging for the subsidies that produced it, the reported number will grow faster than the business beneath it. This does not require fraud. Ordinary optimisation will do.

The e-Conomy SEA 2025 report from Google, Temasek and Bain projects more than US$300 billion of regional GMV in 2025 and US$135 billion of revenue. Its decade summary gives growth multiples of 7.4 times for GMV and 11.2 times for revenue. On the surface, that is what improving monetisation ought to look like. The report also says most food-delivery platforms were profitable or close to it.

Then comes the footnote. The methodology covers six countries from 2016 to 2024, expands to ten in 2025 and annualises the first half of that year. Digital financial services appear in revenue but not in GMV. Dividing 11.2 by 7.4 will not give you a defensible regional take-rate trend. The report also does not test whether tougher investors caused the turn towards profit. I would use it as industry market-sizing work, with the coverage written in the margin, and stop there.

The mistake was never reporting GMV. It was asking GMV to settle a unit-economics argument.

Credit scoring presents a more difficult version of the same problem because even the right target can drift. Consider the claim that 70 percent of Southeast Asia is “unbanked or underbanked”. It bundles account ownership, account use, affordable credit and credit-file coverage into one memorable percentage. They are different things. The World Bank's Global Findex 2025, drawn from national surveys conducted in 2024, reports accounts, payments, saving and borrowing by country. It does not support one permanent regional share for every form of financial exclusion.

There is no need to stretch the denominator. Someone can own an account and still have a thin conventional credit file. Wallet, e-commerce, mobile-money, utility and point-of-sale records may reveal cash flow that a bureau file misses. The IFC's 2026 report Cracking the Credit Code documents how lenders are using such records for underserved borrowers.

A BIS working paper complicates the easy sceptical story. Using transactions from a Chinese fintech, the researchers found that a machine-learning model with non-traditional data predicted defaults and losses better than traditional models, including during the credit-supply shock they examined. It is an encouraging result from one firm, one country and a short window in 2017. I would not carry it much further than that.

A credit model can rank safer and riskier applicants well in its development sample and lose calibration when the applicant pool or economy changes. Accuracy can also coexist with opaque, intrusive or unfair data use. The World Bank and International Committee on Credit Reporting's review treats data quality, consent, privacy and discrimination as separate problems for good reason. A model does not become responsible merely by lifting an accuracy score.

Indonesia offers a useful live example, though not a neat cautionary tale. OJK reported IDR84.66 trillion of outstanding peer-to-peer financing in July 2025 and a TWP90 credit-risk ratio of 2.75 percent. In April 2026, financing had reached IDR102.07 trillion and TWP90 was 4.62 percent. The ratio had moved sharply towards OJK's 5 percent threshold.

Those two observations do not explain the move. Recent loan cohorts may have seasoned; the borrower mix, collections or economy may have changed; underwriting may have weakened. Rapid originations can alter the denominator as well. A sector average hides the question an underwriter actually needs answered: which vintages and approval bands are deteriorating, and by how much under stress?

Nor can enforcement numbers fill in the missing cause. OJK imposed 661 sanctions on online lenders in 2024 and issued four licence-revocation letters, two of them for sanctions. Its February 2025 release says TaniFund and Investree lost their licences after failing to meet minimum-equity requirements and implement supervisory recommendations. That tells us about capital and governance. It tells us little about the accuracy of their borrower scores. The more dramatic interpretation is tempting, but the source does not earn it.

This is where Hubbard earns his keep. Before accepting a metric, write down the decision it is supposed to inform and the level at which the decision changes. Trace how the observed number connects to the outcome you want. Put a range around the estimate, along with the assumptions holding that range together. Decide in advance what evidence would make you abandon the metric. Then ask whether buying one more piece of information has a realistic chance of changing your mind.

Calibration helps with quantities inside boundaries we have drawn. It will not tell us that we drew the wrong map. Small samples can be informative, but Tversky and Kahneman showed in “Belief in the Law of Small Numbers” how readily people treat them as more representative than they are. The same mistake becomes harder to spot when the sample feeds a polished model and a dashboard with two decimal places.

Hubbard's title works as a provocation, not a warranty. Almost anything can be made observable. Far fewer observations deserve to make a decision for us. Good measurement begins with knowing the difference, and with refusing to let a number answer a question it was never built for.
