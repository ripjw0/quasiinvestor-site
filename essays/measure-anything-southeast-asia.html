How to Measure Almost Anything in Southeast Asia

Douglas Hubbard says you can measure your way to better decisions. In Southeast Asia's startups, that discipline is the most useful import and the most dangerous one.

Douglas Hubbard's How to Measure Anything makes a claim that sounds reckless and mostly holds up. The things a manager files under unmeasurable are usually just undefined. Ask what the word means, ask what you would see if there were more or less of it, and the fog resolves into something you can observe and put a number to. Measurement, in his telling, aims lower than certainty. It is any observation that reduces uncertainty enough to change what you would do. The discipline that follows is quieter than the title. You should measure the one thing that would change your mind, and you should not spend much on measuring anything else.

That idea travels, and where it lands hardest is Southeast Asia, among the founders building in markets that are large, young, and short on the clean historical data the method assumes. For them Hubbard is at once the most useful book on the shelf and the most dangerous, because the same region that rewards disciplined measurement also punishes the false confidence measurement can breed. The interesting question is not whether to measure. It is knowing which numbers deserve trust, and which ones are lying to you with a straight face.

The parts of the book worth carrying are practical. Decomposition takes a frightening quantity and splits it into pieces small enough to estimate, and the splitting usually reveals you knew more than you feared. Calibration training teaches people to give ninety percent ranges that are right about ninety percent of the time, which is harder than it sounds, since almost everyone is overconfident until they practice. A small random sample beats the zero that most decisions actually run on. A range beats a single guess, because a plan built on one middle number tends to break in the places the number hid. None of this is exotic, and Philip Tetlock's forecasting research shows that the calibration piece is a trainable skill rather than a gift. The trouble starts when the method meets a market that refuses to behave.

Consider the number Southeast Asian technology told its story with for a decade. Gross merchandise value, the total worth of everything transacted on a platform, became the headline founders pitched and investors rewarded. It has the shape of progress, up and to the right, and it is easy to grow, which is exactly the problem. You can buy GMV with vouchers, free delivery, and cash back, and much of the region did. It is the kind of measure Hubbard warns against, one that rarely maps to the decision that actually matters, which is whether the business can make money once the subsidies stop.

Charles Goodhart's law is the older name for what comes next. When a measure becomes a target, it stops measuring the thing it stood for, because everyone starts optimizing the number instead. Jerry Muller's The Tyranny of Metrics is a catalogue of the same failure across medicine, policing, and finance, places where a scorecard arrived and judgment quietly left. In Southeast Asia the scorecard was GMV, and for years it decided who raised, who merged, and who got to be called the winner.

The correction, when it came, looked a lot like Hubbard proven in the negative. The e-Conomy SEA 2025 report from Google, Temasek, and Bain, now in its tenth year, puts the region's digital economy past three hundred billion dollars in GMV. Read past the headline and the real story is the second number. Across the decade GMV grew about 7.4 times while revenue grew 11.2 times, and revenue is set to reach roughly a hundred and thirty-five billion dollars as profitability finally accelerates. The region's largest platforms, the ones behind the ride-hailing, delivery, and shopping apps most of Southeast Asia opens every day, crossed or approached profitability only once the market stopped paying for growth alone and started asking about unit economics. The measure that would have changed the decision was sitting there the whole time. It was just less flattering to report.

GMV is not worthless. The trouble is that the easy number and the decisive number are rarely the same, and the gap between them is where a great deal of Southeast Asian capital was quietly lost. Hubbard would tell a founder to find the measurement with a real inflection point, the one where a result on this side means build and a result on that side means stop. GMV almost never has one. Contribution margin usually does. That the definitive regional report still leads with the GMV headline, a full decade in, is a small monument to how hard the flattering number is to give up.

If GMV shows Hubbard's discipline honored in the breach, digital lending shows his optimism at full stretch, doing real good and courting real danger at once. Start with the good. Something close to seventy percent of Southeast Asia is unbanked or underbanked, which means most adults have no credit file for a lender to read. In Hubbard's terms the borrower is not unmeasurable, only undefined. Decompose creditworthiness into things you can observe and the signals are everywhere. How someone tops up a phone, pays a utility bill, uses a wallet, or handles a device. Lenders now assemble a usable risk estimate from this alternative data, often with the machine learning that the latest e-Conomy report places at the center of the region's next chapter. A thin, imperfect sample of behaviour beats the blank file it replaces, and at its best this is measurement widening access to credit rather than guarding the gate. It is the most hopeful idea in the book made concrete.

Now the danger, which wears the same clothes. The confidence that you can measure anyone's creditworthiness runs straight into the objections Hubbard is quickest to wave off. A model trained on a few years of a rising market has seen only good weather. It learns that defaults are rare and roughly independent, and both assumptions fail at exactly the wrong moment. In a downturn defaults arrive together, correlated by the same shock, and the tidy confidence interval built in calm conditions blows through. This is the fat-tailed world Nassim Taleb calls the ludic fallacy, the error of treating an open, messy market as if it were a casino with the odds printed on the table. Early loan cohorts make it worse. A few thousand clean repayments feel like proof, when Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman showed long ago that small samples invite belief in patterns that are really just the wobble of a small draw. And Goodhart waits at the end, because the moment a score becomes the gate, borrowers and fraud rings learn to farm it.

Indonesia has been living the cautionary version. Its peer-to-peer lending sector grew fast on exactly this promise, then met rising defaults, deliberate non-payment, and outright fraud. The regulator has placed a string of platforms under special supervision for capital shortfalls and bad loans, and the wreckage includes a lender whose ninety-day default rate passed sixty percent before its licence was pulled, and another that raised hundreds of millions of dollars before collapsing amid allegations against its founder. These are not failures of too little measurement. They are failures of measuring something real, an early signal that looked solid, and mistaking it for the thing that would have changed the decision, which was how the loan book behaves when the weather turns. The number was honest. The confidence wrapped around it was not.

None of this argues against measuring. It argues for reading Hubbard the way Southeast Asia should, taking the discipline and refusing the swagger. The discipline is close to essential here, more than in the mature markets the book was written for, because the temptation to steer by a flattering proxy is strongest where real data is scarce and the pressure to show growth never lets up. Measure the one thing that would change your decision. State your uncertainty as a range and mean it. Never let the easy number stand in for the decisive one. A founder who absorbs only that will make better calls than most of the people funding them.

The swagger is the part to leave on the page. Anything is a promise the method cannot keep in a market with fat tails, thin histories, and metrics that people game for a living. The honest version of the title is smaller and more useful. You cannot measure anything. You can measure a great deal more than you assumed, and the whole art is knowing which measurement would move you, then being brave enough to want the answer even when it says stop. In a region that has spent a decade learning the difference between a big number and a real one, that is not a modest lesson. It is close to the whole game.
