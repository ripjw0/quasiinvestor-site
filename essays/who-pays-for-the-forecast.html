Who Pays for the Forecast

Korea cut off Polymarket at the ISP level. Whatever information these markets produce, it does not settle the prior question of what kind of commercial activity is paying for it.

On 18 August 2026 the telecommunications deliberation subcommittee of the Korea Communications Standards Commission resolved on a corrective request ordering domestic providers to block access to Polymarket. The decision did not arrive from nowhere. According to Wikitree's account the commission opened its formal review in May and heard Polymarket's position from 6 July, a date Media Today also reports, voting to block six weeks after that. Before deciding, it took views from the police, the state commission that supervises the gambling industry and the Korea Sports Promotion Foundation, all of which said Polymarket's method of operation could amount to gambling or to opening a gambling place under domestic law, Yonhap reported. Whatever else it was, this was an inter-agency classification exercise with a hearing attached.

The reflex reading in Western and crypto coverage is that this is censorship, and futile censorship, since a VPN defeats a network-level block. The observation is correct and it settles nothing. Whether a block can be circumvented is a question about enforcement yield. Whether the thing being blocked was correctly classified is a different question, and the first does not answer the second. Singapore, which has run this playbook for years, concedes the point outright. Answering a parliamentary question in January 2025, Home Affairs Minister K. Shanmugam reported more than 3,800 illegal gambling websites and more than 145,000 associated transactions worth roughly $37 million blocked as of 31 December 2024, and said in the same reply that the measures had “impeded access to illegal online gambling to some extent” while the approach was “clearly not foolproof as illegal operators can easily create new websites”. Singapore has gone on blocking. Imperfect friction at the network layer is one instrument among several.

What makes the Korean decision worth arguing about is what the commission is reported to have examined rather than what it concluded. The decision document and the subcommittee minutes are not public, so everything that follows is the reasoning as Korean outlets report it. On SBS's English account, three grounds carry the decision. The first is payoff structure, where the commission found a winner-take-all profit-and-loss structure tied to chance and to events users cannot influence, which it said fosters a speculative mindset. The second is what was on offer to whom, with markets on domestic-specific questions, the August rainfall total in Seoul being the example that recurs across Korean reporting. The third is the operator, and it is the most consequential of the three. Despite the decentralization claim, SBS reports the commission concluding that Polymarket's operators manage and control the platform as a whole, including opening markets and setting trading rules, and generate economic profit by collecting transaction fees, with deposit, withdrawal and settlement running through crypto. ZDNet Korea carries the same finding in the same terms.

On the law, Korean reporting is consistent that the commission characterized the service as information provided for the purpose of aiding gambling or of opening a place for gambling under the Criminal Act, and as information for the purpose of similar acts under the National Sports Promotion Act. The statutes themselves are public and unglamorous. The Criminal Act treats gambling at Article 246 and the setting up of places for gambling at Article 247. Article 26 of the National Sports Promotion Act provides that no person or entity except the Seoul Olympic Sports Promotion Foundation and its entrusted operator may issue sports promotion betting tickets or engage in any activities similar thereto. The corrective-request power itself sits in the Act that established the commission, keyed to the categories of illegal information defined under the Network Act, under which providers can be required, after deliberation, to refuse, suspend or restrict specified information. None of it was drafted with blockchains in mind, which is rather the point. A regulator working from a decades-old statute is forced to ask what the activity is before it can ask what to call it.

Polymarket's answer, stated fairly, was that it had removed Korean-language service and supported no won payments, so the domestic network rules should not reach it, and that it neither collects nor manages user funds directly nor issues sports betting tickets, since trades are non-custodial and settle peer to peer through smart contracts, so the statutory elements were unmet. The commission rejected both limbs together, saying, as Media Today and Kyunghyang Shinmun report it, that the application of domestic law cannot be evaded on the grounds of technical characteristics such as decentralization or service choices such as whether a Korean-language interface is offered, where the service provides a substantive illegal gambling environment to domestic users.

The sector's first defense is that these are information markets, and it deserves to be taken at full strength before it is taken apart. Prediction markets do forecast well. Comparing Iowa Electronic Markets prices with 964 national polls across US presidential elections from 1988 to 2004, Berg, Nelson and Rietz found the market closer to the eventual outcome 74 percent of the time. Nothing in the Korean decision disturbs that, and nobody should want it disturbed.

What has changed is that researchers have finally looked at who inside these markets produces the accuracy and who pays for it. Working with every Polymarket trade from 2023 to 2025, across 1.72 million accounts, 210,322 markets and $13.76 billion of volume, Gómez-Cram, Guo, Jensen and Kung find the forecasting quality comes from a persistently skilled minority of roughly 3 percent of accounts, who react to public news as it arrives and trade against the crowd's behavioural mistakes. The crowd generates most of the volume and little of the information. At the other end of the same population, 67 percent of those accounts are classified as unlucky or unskilled losers, and they absorb the platform's entire pool of aggregate losses. Yale's summary of the work puts it flatly, saying that the remaining majority does not produce accuracy, it funds it. A separate study by Akey, Grégoire, Harvie and Martineau, running through 588 million trades and $67 billion of volume from November 2022 to March 2026, finds the gains concentrated to the point of caricature, with the top 1 percent of users who finished in profit capturing 76.5 percent of all profits, and the winners systematically supplying liquidity through limit orders while the losers take it with market orders.

Set the commission's operator finding beside those numbers and the chain is complete and entirely mundane. Participants put money at risk on mutually exclusive outcomes. A small skilled minority captures gains funded by a much larger population of less-skilled participants. On the commission's account, the operator, having established the marketplace and written its rules, takes a fee on the flow. That is the commercial activity a regulator is being asked to classify, and the forecasting value is a byproduct of it. In the market we actually observe, broad participation is economically load-bearing. It is not epistemically load-bearing, and an argument that defends the epistemics has not yet said anything about the economics.

The second defense is decentralization, and it is the one that invites more blocks rather than fewer. A venue that cannot be licensed or supervised, and cannot be compelled to exclude minors and self-excluded gamblers, has removed the regulator's most direct instruments and left the blunt ones, at the network layer and against payments, advertising and intermediaries. The defense selects for exactly the response it received. Nor was that response improvised. In February 2026 Korea amended the rules governing its deliberation body to route serious categories of illegal information, gambling explicitly among them, into a simplified written review built for faster blocking. The machinery was rebuilt months before Polymarket needed it.

None of which makes gambling the uniquely correct answer everywhere, and the American record is the reason to say so plainly. On the same contract form, the federal posture has moved the other way. In February 2026 the CFTC withdrew the restrictive 2024 event-contracts proposal, saying it would advance a new rulemaking grounded in a coherent reading of the Commodity Exchange Act. In March it opened an advance notice of proposed rulemaking on prediction markets, the notice recording that applications for exchange registration had more than doubled over the past year, largely from entities interested primarily or exclusively in running prediction markets. In June it proposed a contract-by-contract framework asking first whether a contract involves an enumerated activity such as gaming, war or terrorism, and then whether it is contrary to the public interest. Throughout, the Commission has asserted what it calls its clear and longstanding exclusive jurisdiction over event contracts, suing several states that applied state gambling law to registered exchanges and, on 11 August, invoking emergency authority to keep an exchange operating amid the New York fight.

That is a posture. In late July a federal judge in Minnesota preliminarily blocked that state's prediction-market ban, finding the challengers likely to succeed on preemption while noting that not every wager on such a platform would necessarily qualify as a swap under the Commodity Exchange Act. The states are still fighting, and the unresolved fight is better evidence than a clean verdict would be. Washington increasingly asks the prediction-market question inside federal derivatives law; Korea asked it inside gambling and illegal-information law. The same object now sits inside two regulatory perimeters.

It is tempting to reach for a tidy test that separates a real derivative from a wager by asking whether anyone holds an underlying exposure to hedge. The CFTC's own materials rule that out. Its public explainer says event contracts can be used to hedge, offering a citrus farmer buying a weather contract against a freeze, and says in the next breath that they also provide a way for traders to seek profits by taking risks, or speculating. One instrument on one venue under one regulator serves both purposes. The vocabulary question and the classification question are separate, which is precisely why two competent authorities can examine one object and file it in different places.

India shows the sequence most clearly, because it ran through both perimeters in a year. In April 2025 SEBI cautioned the public about opinion trading platforms whose payout depends on the outcome of a yes or no proposition, observed that some resemble investment platforms by borrowing terms such as profits, stop loss and trading, and stated that opinion trading falls outside SEBI's purview because what is traded is not a security, so no securities-market investor protection applies. Financial vocabulary did not put the activity inside financial regulation. India then answered the question SEBI had declined. The Promotion and Regulation of Online Gaming Act, 2025, assented to on 22 August 2025 and operational from 1 May 2026 when its implementing rules commenced, defines an online money game as one played by paying fees or depositing money or other stakes in expectation of winning monetary or other enrichment, whether the game is based on skill, chance or both. It prohibits offering such games, advertising them and facilitating financial transactions for them, and makes information relating to a non-compliant online money gaming service liable to be blocked for access by the public.

Singapore's contribution is architecture. Its Gambling Control Act 2022 works prohibition-first, with gambling services unlawful unless licensed or exempted, and the enforcement stack of website blocking, payment blocking and advertising restriction already built. In May 2026 the Home Affairs Ministry described the same set of tools ahead of the World Cup. Indonesia had already reached for its own. In May 2026 its digital ministry cut off Polymarket as online gambling in disguise, three months before Korea, which makes the Korean decision a second Asian instance. Where a jurisdiction already classifies the activity as gambling, a prediction market can plug into an enforcement stack built for online gambling years earlier. Where it does not, as India shows, lawmakers may widen the perimeter explicitly.

Which leaves the argument the sector has not made and could. Concede the gambling character, then argue that regulated gambling with an information byproduct is worth licensing, on the ordinary terms that come with it, meaning KYC, exclusion registers, tax, advertising limits and audited settlement. In a region that already licenses lotteries and state-run sports pools, that argument has a plausible path that “we are not gambling” does not. Why it goes unmade is conjecture, and worth marking as such. The obvious guess is that conceding the character would import the whole licensing burden, the consumer-protection obligations, and a valuation frame in which these businesses are compared with gambling operators rather than with exchanges. That is a hypothesis about incentives. It is not a finding about anyone's motives.

It is a thesis that can be broken, which is what makes it worth stating. An Asian regulator licensing a prediction market as a derivative, or admitting one to a sandbox, would falsify it. So would a carve-out for institutional-only or non-monetary forecasting venues, which would show that the objection was to the wagering rather than to the forecasting. So would Polymarket geofencing Korea voluntarily and being unblocked, which would show the classification was negotiable all along. On the American side, a definitive preemption ruling either way would move things. A clean federal win sharpens the divergence between the two perimeters; a state win shows both of them operating inside one country.

The information these markets produce is real. What the evidence does not establish is that mass participation is the source of that information; in the market we observe, it appears instead to be part of the mechanism that pays for it. What is genuinely at stake is that commercial activity, and it is the thing the sector rarely defends on its own terms. Korea looked at a payoff structure it called winner-take-all and at an operator writing the rules and taking a fee, and asked what to call the result before accepting the label on the box. The research adds something the commission did not have: roughly 67 percent of accounts are classified as unlucky or unskilled losers, while only 3.14 percent qualify as skilled. That is who pays for the forecast. Nothing here is investment advice.
